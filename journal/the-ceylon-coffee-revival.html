The Ceylon Coffee Revival: Sri Lanka's first great crop returns.

Published 13 October 2026 Reading 4 min

Long before Ceylon tea filled the world's cups, the island grew coffee. It was Sri Lanka's first great export crop — and one of the largest coffee origins on earth — before a blight erased it almost overnight. Epitome is part of its quiet return.

An island of coffee

In the middle of the nineteenth century, Ceylon was among the greatest coffee producers in the world. Highland slopes that would later hold tea were planted with Arabica, and the island drank in a fortune on the strength of the bean. The crop drew planters, built estates, and gave Ceylon a place on the global map — the same place tea would later inherit.

The rust that changed everything

Then came Hemileia vastatrix — coffee leaf rust. In the 1860s it swept the plantations, and within a decade the industry had collapsed. The planters did not abandon the land; they changed crops, to Camellia sinensis. Ceylon tea became the name the world remembers, and coffee — the island's first crop — was nearly forgotten.

What survived

The coffee never entirely vanished. Higher still, in the central highlands, small shade-grown plots of Arabica survived inside biodiverse Kandyan gardens — coffee beside pepper, clove, and nutmeg — tended by farming families for generations. It was never monoculture, never blended away. It simply waited, between 1,200 and 1,800 metres, where nights are cool and the morning sits in mist.

The return

The specialty coffee movement changed the calculus. As the world began to prize single origins again, the rarity of Ceylon Arabica — high-grown, shade-grown, balanced — became its value. Epitome is that crop, kept as a single expression: one 500 g bag of whole-bean, medium-roast Arabica. No blend to soften the soil. No second roast to hide a fault.

The Ceylon name already travels. The bean does not need a blend to introduce itself.

To drink Epitome is to taste the island's first story — the one it told before tea.
